Methodology

The SSPI scores what governments do, not how countries happen to be doing.

This page documents how the index is built: which countries and years it covers, how policy indicators are chosen, where the underlying data comes from, how raw measurements become comparable scores, and how those scores are combined into a single number. Each step is a methodological commitment. We state them here so that readers can weigh them, disagree with them, and test alternatives against the same data.

{{ section_head(1, 'overview', 'What the Index Measures') }}

The Sustainable and Shared-Prosperity Policy Index documents the policies practiced around the world that build an economy caring for people and the planet. We largely know which policies create a sustainable world, share prosperity, provide a comfortable and meaningful life, and reduce suffering globally. The question the index asks is narrower and more answerable: to what extent are governments actually adopting them? Following Amartya Sen (1999), we presume the goal is to guarantee a comfortable, dignified life with full opportunities for everyone, now and in future generations, and we read a country's policies as the measure of how far its government has committed to that goal.

That question sets the unit of measurement. The SSPI scores policy—what a government has legislated, funded, built, protected, or refused to do—rather than national wellbeing, growth, or happiness. Outcomes depend on geography, history, trading partners, and luck. Policy is the part a government controls, and the part another government can copy.

The index is a tree with four levels. At the leaves sit {{ indicator_count }} indicators, each a single measurable feature of national policy. Indicators are grouped into {{ category_count }} categories, each a package of policies that works together in one sector. Categories are grouped into {{ pillar_count }} pillars corresponding to three core tasks of government, and the pillars combine into the SSPI itself. Every item at every level, from a single indicator up to the index, is expressed on the same 0 to 1 scale, where 0 is the weakest policy the scale admits and 1 is the strongest.

A score is a statement about policy, not about welfare. A country can score well on a policy that has not yet produced results, and badly on an outcome it enjoys for reasons that have nothing to do with its government.

The methodology summarized on this page is developed at length, together with the analysis it supports, in the SSPI working paper: Brown and Misko (2025), National Policies to Support Sustainable, Equitable Economies, IRLE Working Paper No. 101-25.

{{ section_head(2, 'scope', 'Countries and Years') }}

The SSPI is calculated for {{ country_count }} countries: the {{ core_country_count }} countries of the original panel—the members of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the Group of Twenty, and five further countries classified as high income by the World Bank—together with {{ extended_country_count }} countries added as coverage improved. These are the largest and most industrialized economies, for which most policy data are available and reliable, and together they account for the large majority of world economic output, of world population, and of global greenhouse gas emissions.

Scores are computed annually from {{ year_start }} through {{ year_end }}, giving a balanced panel of country-years. The panel is balanced by construction rather than by luck: source series begin and end at different dates, and the gaps are filled by the imputation procedure described below.

The country list is a compromise, and it is worth naming what the compromise costs. Policy data are dense where statistical agencies are well funded and thin everywhere else. Extending the panel further down the income distribution would mean publishing more imputation than measurement, so we have preferred a smaller panel we can defend to a larger one we cannot. Conclusions drawn from the SSPI apply to the countries in it and should not be extrapolated beyond them.

The full list, with the group each country belongs to, is on the countries page.

{{ section_head(3, 'indicators', 'Building Policy Indicators') }}

Indicator design rests on a large body of literature describing and analyzing government policies and programs, with particular attention to best practice across countries and to the policies supporting the Sustainable Development Goals. Examples of the work behind specific indicators include labor market policy and its effect on labor market operation (Card and Oreopoulos 2019), policies protecting groundwater alongside sustainable water provision (GlobeScan 2019), and land management policy for natural carbon sequestration in forests (Ni et al 2016).

Direct measures and policy proxies

In designing policy indicators we opt for the most direct measurement of policy for which reliable data exist. Where possible, direct measurements of policy are used: Biodiversity Protection (the proportion of ecologically important areas protected by law), Tax Revenue (revenue raised as a share of GDP), and the government expenditure component of research and development (the share of GDP spent on public R&D). Direct numerical measurement is possible only when a government controls the level of a single parameter that determines the strength of a policy.

Many of the policies needed for sustainable and shared prosperity are not so simple. Some policy goals require a multifaceted package adapted to local circumstances and constraints, whose parts work together to achieve the goal. In those cases we use outcome metrics as proxies for packages of government policy. Deforestation (percentage change in forest cover against a 1990s benchmark), Fatal Injuries (fatal injuries per ten thousand workers), and Primary School Enrollment (the share of primary-age children enrolled in formal education) are not set directly by a policy dial in the way public research spending is, yet government policy nevertheless determines their level, especially over the long run. Proxy measures acknowledge the variety of specific policies—each adapted to a particular geographic, cultural, and political context—that countries may pursue toward a universal policy goal.

Proxies are the largest interpretive risk in the index. An outcome metric picks up policy and circumstance together, and the two cannot be fully separated. We take the trade-off knowingly: a proxy that captures policy imperfectly is more informative than omitting a policy area that resists direct measurement.

Organizing indicators into categories and pillars

The three pillars correspond to three tasks that governments in the twenty-first century are asked to perform. Public Goods covers goods and services supplied or guaranteed directly by government, which are largely under the direct control of a national or regional administration. Market Structure brings together the policies that regulate and structure how markets function, on the supply side through employment policy, taxation, and the protection of property and competition, and on the demand side through economic security and a financial system that supports the effective operation of markets. The policies in those two pillars do not capture a government's responsibility to protect the environment for people today and for future generations, which is the role of the Sustainability pillar: policies governing the management and use of natural resources and the externalities that follow from degrading a country's natural capital. A firm's production decisions rest on its own costs, which typically exclude external costs to public health or the environment; the public pays those costs instead. The Sustainability pillar measures the extent to which governments have policies in place that internalize them.

Pillars and categories are best read as systems of policies that operate together rather than as lists of separable instruments. Many kinds of policy affect how markets operate, or the health of the environment, or the quality of life, and the goal is a set of policies that work on those things jointly. Infrastructure is a fair example: access to electricity, water quality, basic sanitation, internet access, and the transport network are commonly built by governments and either operated by them or regulated in private hands, and together they support daily life and shape environmental outcomes at the same time.

Every indicator, with its definition, its sources, and its score function, is listed in the indicator table.

{{ section_head(4, 'data-sources', 'Selecting Data Sources') }}

In selecting data to represent the policy indicators, and then aggregating them into a composite index, we follow the recommended practice for composite indicators set out by the OECD (OECD 2008). Indicators are built on data that are well defined, reliable, and publicly available over time. We use only credible organizations whose data cover many countries. Objective administrative data is preferred to subjective survey data.

Where two candidate variables might represent the same information, and so be interchangeable, we compare their correlation. When variables are highly correlated and judged to carry the same information, we keep the one with more country observations or higher data quality. We also use sensitivity testing to establish how much an indicator depends on the particular variable chosen and on the way it enters its category.

The index currently draws on {{ dataset_count }} datasets from {{ organization_count }} organizations. A dataset is one measured series from one source, collected and cleaned on its own terms; an indicator is one or more datasets combined by a published score function. Some indicators are a single dataset renamed; others average several. Biodiversity Protection, for instance, is the average of the protected share of important terrestrial, freshwater, and marine sites, each scored separately first.

Each dataset has its own page recording its source organization, its query, its unit, and the code that collects it. Those pages are reachable from the indicator table, and the cleaned series themselves can be taken whole from the download page.

{{ section_head(5, 'scoring', 'Scoring with Goalposts') }}

Every indicator is set up so that a higher score represents a policy oriented toward a better outcome, and every indicator is normalized to a value between 0 and 1, so that widely varying data are expressed in comparable units. Normalization uses a pair of goalposts: a lower goalpost representing a hypothetical minimum for the policy variable and an upper goalpost representing a hypothetical maximum. A raw value is placed on that scale, and anything beyond either post is held at the end of the scale.

This follows the goalposting used for the Human Development Index, whose dimension indices—the equivalent of our indicator scores—are normalized to values between 0 and 1 against minima and maxima set from both historical evidence and achievability.

Inverting an indicator by swapping its goalposts

Some policy variables run the wrong way: a higher murder rate, a higher Gini coefficient, or higher energy intensity all indicate weaker policy. Rather than carry a separate direction flag, the SSPI inverts these indicators by putting the larger value in the lower-goalpost position. The formula is unchanged and the slope simply runs downhill. The Gini coefficient after taxes and transfers, for example, is scored with goalposts of 70 and 20, so a Gini of 70 scores 0 and a Gini of 20 or better scores 1. Where the goalposts come from

Setting the goalposts of an indicator shifts the center and controls the spread of its score distribution while preserving the ranks in the observed data. Where an internationally established norm exists, we use it—several goalposts follow the 2022 Sustainable Development Report (Sachs et al 2022). Where no norm exists, the historical values observed across countries guide what is possible for high performers while leaving room for future improvement, and also benchmark weak performance. We use sensitivity testing on the goalposts of an indicator to examine the distribution and its outliers across countries, and take observed high values as a benchmark of what good policy can achieve.

For biodiversity policy, benchmarks can be drawn from environmental science. To protect the planet's ecosystems, nations must stop causing extinctions and preserve species. The share of important sites covered by protected areas runs from 0 to 100 percent of internationally recognized sites, and a goal of 100 percent is reasonable, since ideally a country's policy would protect every known important site.

Carbon sequestration is harder. We do not know the maximum amount of carbon a country could sequester, because it varies widely with geography and cross-country data are sparse. Without clear benchmarks, we rely on the data itself—land characteristics and historical trends—to set goalposts for what good policy can achieve. Country policy is ranked on carbon density, the ratio of carbon stock in living biomass to forest land, measured against that country's own 1990s average, when deforestation became conspicuous. Higher values indicate that a country is allocating land to forests that sequester carbon. The benchmark is the country's own land endowment rather than a universal target. Deforestation follows the same logic, using the ratio of naturally regenerating forest to the 1990s average.

Both extremely high and extremely low outliers are censored and assigned the value of the nearer goalpost. Setting the lower goalpost too low would penalize countries constrained by resources, geography, or history beyond the reach of policy, so some lower goalposts deliberately sit above the worst observed value, both to suppress noise and to mark a reasonable floor for weak policy. A few very high values were judged to be noise and are capped at the upper goalpost. This mirrors the SDG Index, which sets upper targets from known goals or from the average of top performers, sets lower targets at the 2.5th percentile, and censors each distribution at both ends.

One pair of goalposts applies to every country and every year, so a score of 0.60 carries the same meaning in {{ year_start }} as in {{ year_end }}. That is what makes the panel comparable over time, and it is also why goalposts are not revised casually: moving one rewrites the history of the indicator.

{{ section_head(6, 'imputation', 'Filling Gaps in the Panel') }}

Source series rarely cover every country in every year. Without some treatment of the gaps, the index would be missing wherever a single contributing series was late, early, or absent, and countries would drop in and out of the ranking for reasons that have nothing to do with their policies. Imputation fills those gaps so that each indicator has a complete set of country-year observations across {{ year_start }} to {{ year_end }}.

Three methods are applied, in this order:

  • Backward extrapolation carries the earliest observed value back to the start of the panel.
  • Forward extrapolation carries the latest observed value forward to the end of the panel.
  • Linear interpolation fills gaps between two observations by drawing a straight line between them.

Imputed values are then scored with the same goalposts as observed values, and stored separately from them, so that any number in the index can be traced back to whether it was measured or inferred. Indicators with complete coverage are never imputed at all.

Imputation moves information; it does not create any. Flat extrapolation is the conservative choice—it assumes policy did not change rather than inventing a trend—but it understates real change at the ends of a series, and a long run of extrapolated years should be read as an assumption rather than as a measurement.

After imputation, a country-year is scored only if every indicator is present for it. A country with an irreparable hole in one indicator is left out of the index for that year rather than being scored on a partial tree, which would make its score incomparable with everyone else's.

{{ section_head(7, 'aggregation', 'Aggregating to the Index') }}

Aggregation is deliberately the least clever part of the methodology. Each level of the tree is an unweighted arithmetic mean of the level below it. A category is the average of its indicators, a pillar is the average of its categories, and the SSPI is the average of its {{ pillar_count }} pillars. There are no coefficients, no principal components, and no expert weights.

Our starting position was to use equal weights because they are widely used and because they attenuate bias and arbitrariness. Equal weights imply that all policy indicators are equally important within a category, all categories are equally important within a pillar, and the {{ pillar_count }} pillars are equally important within the SSPI. This reflects our assumption that the SSPI integrates a system of interrelated and indivisible policies that are equally important, none of which a government may neglect on the grounds that it is doing well elsewhere. The SDG Index takes the same position, treating each Sustainable Development Goal equally with fixed equal weights.

Equal weights are still weights

Averaging at each level is not the same as weighting every indicator equally in the index. Because a category divides its share among however many indicators it holds, and a pillar divides its share among however many categories it holds, an indicator's influence on the final score depends on how crowded its neighborhood is.

We publish this table rather than bury it. Equal weighting is the honest default when there is no defensible basis for declaring one policy area more important than another, and it keeps every judgment in the structure of the tree, where a reader can see it, instead of in a vector of coefficients, where they cannot. But equal weighting is itself a choice, and the table is what that choice costs. Adding an indicator to a category dilutes the indicators already there; splitting a category in two doubles the weight of its contents.

The right response to a weighting scheme you doubt is to test it. We have done some of that testing ourselves: sensitivity tests on the weights at both levels of aggregation—pillars and categories—compared country rankings under alternative schemes, including unequal weights and the geometric average, against the rankings achieved under equal weighting, and found little difference in rankings or scores between the geometric and simple averages. The custom index builder lets you go further and rebuild the tree—drop indicators, move them between categories, split or merge categories, change score functions—score your version against the same underlying data, and compare the result with the published index.

One difference is worth knowing before you read your own results. The published index scores a country-year only when every indicator is present, while the custom builder averages whichever children are available. A custom tree with sparse coverage will therefore still produce a score, and that score is an average over a different set of indicators for different countries.

{{ section_head(8, 'limitations', 'What the Index Cannot Tell You') }}

Composite indices invite more confidence than they earn. These are the limitations we consider most likely to mislead a careful reader.

  • Policy effort is not policy quality, and proxies blur the two. Where an indicator is an outcome metric standing in for a package of policies, it captures circumstance alongside policy. Forest cover reflects geography and history as well as land management.
  • Equal weighting is not neutral. As the table above shows, the implied weight of an indicator varies by a factor of about {{ spread }} depending on where it sits in the tree.
  • Clipping destroys information at the ends. Every country beyond a goalpost receives an identical 0 or 1, so the index cannot distinguish among leaders or among laggards on an indicator where many countries are censored.
  • Imputed years are assumptions. Flat extrapolation across a long gap will show a country's policy as unchanged when it may not have been.
  • The panel is not the world. The {{ country_count }} countries are rich and large economies. Relationships found in this panel need not hold outside it.
  • Small differences are not differences. Scores inherit the measurement error of every source series beneath them. Gaps of a few hundredths—and the rank changes that follow from them—should not be treated as findings.
  • Indicator selection encodes a view of good policy. Deciding that a policy area belongs in the index, and which measurable feature represents it, is a judgment made by the research team. We document those judgments rather than claim not to have made any.
  • Source data is revised. Statistical agencies restate historical series. A score can change between collections without any policy having changed, which is why each published figure is tied to the collection that produced it.
{{ section_head(9, 'reproducibility', 'Reproducibility') }}

For its first years the SSPI lived in a stack of spreadsheets. An assignment might be to find a data reporter's website and copy the numbers by hand into a sheet; an indicator would be computed in a workbook, the result copied by hand into the main file, and the workbook and the source promptly lost. The method worked, but it had a fatal property: the only way to check that the data had been processed correctly was to process it again by hand and hope the two agreed.

Two failure modes recurred. Rankings were often produced by selecting a range and sorting it rather than by a ranking function, and a selection that was slightly wrong scrambled countries against their values. And the scoring scheme was distributed across dozens of sheets, so answering “which indicators actually went into this category?” meant auditing every formula in every row. Both classes of error were hard to find, costly to fix, and a standing threat to the reliability of published findings.

Writing the index as code fixed the underlying problem, because code forces tacit knowledge to become explicit. Sources and transformations are named once and run repeatedly; a mistake is a mistake in a written procedure, and so it can be found. The 2018 index was frozen only after it had been reproduced in code, and that reproduction caught several substantive errors in earlier versions.

Data now moves through the system in stages—collected from the source API, cleaned into a standard form, imputed where necessary, scored against goalposts, and finalized into item scores—and every stage is stored rather than overwritten. Any number on this site can be walked backwards to the response the source organization returned.
