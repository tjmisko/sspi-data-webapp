Methodology

Measuring and Scoring National Policies

The SSPI measures the strength of the national policies that support sustainable and shared prosperity. Each policy indicator is scored between 0 and 1 against fixed goalposts. Indicator scores are aggregated by unweighted averages into category, pillar, and index scores.

{{ section_head('overview') }}

The Sustainable and Shared-Prosperity Policy Index (SSPI) documents the national policies that build an economy caring for people and the planet. A large literature in economics and climate science describes the policies that create a sustainable world, share prosperity, and provide a comfortable and meaningful life. The SSPI asks to what extent governments have adopted them. Following Sen (1999), we presume the goal of policy is to guarantee a comfortable, dignified life with full opportunities for everyone, now and in future generations, and we read a country's policies as the measure of its government's commitment to that goal.

The unit of measurement is policy: what a government has legislated, funded, built, regulated, or protected. Many indices compare the economic performance of countries in terms of inequality, greenhouse gas emissions, health, education, and productivity. To our knowledge, no other index measures, across a broad range of policy areas, the national policies created to guide and support those outcomes. Outcomes depend on geography, history, trading partners, and circumstance. Policy is the component a government controls and the component another government can adopt.

The index has four levels. Each of the {{ indicator_count }} indicators measures a single feature of national policy. Indicators are grouped into {{ category_count }} categories, each a package of policies that work together in one sector. Categories are grouped into {{ pillar_count }} pillars corresponding to three functions of government: protecting the environment, structuring markets, and delivering public goods and services. The pillars combine into the SSPI. Every item at every level is expressed on the same scale from 0 to 1, where 0 represents a very weak policy and 1 represents an ideal policy.

Scores describe policy. A country may score well on a policy whose effects have not yet appeared in outcome data, and poorly on an outcome it enjoys for reasons unrelated to its government.

{{ section_head('scope') }}

The SSPI is calculated for {{ country_count }} countries: the {{ core_country_count }} countries of the original panel (the members of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the Group of Twenty, and five further countries classified as high income by the World Bank) together with {{ extended_country_count }} countries added as coverage improved. These are the largest and most industrialized economies, for which most policy data are available and reliable, and together they account for the large majority of world economic output, of world population, and of global greenhouse gas emissions.

Scores are computed annually from {{ year_start }} through {{ year_end }}, giving a balanced panel of country-years. To handle variations in the availability of data and series coverage, we fill gaps in the series by imputation (see details below).

The country list is a compromise. Policy and proxy data are highly available where statistical agencies are well-funded, but they are scarce elsewhere. Extending the panel for countries across the developing world (for whom policy choices and institution building may be of the greatest consequence and therefore most important to study carefully) relies on more imputation than measurement. Conclusions drawn from the SSPI apply to the countries in it and should not be extrapolated beyond them.

The full list, with the group each country belongs to, is on the countries page.

{{ section_head('indicators') }}

Indicator design rests on a large body of literature describing and analyzing government policies and programs, with particular attention to best practice across countries and to the policies supporting the Sustainable Development Goals. Examples of the work behind specific indicators include labor market policy and its effect on labor market operation (Card and Oreopoulos 2019), policies protecting groundwater alongside sustainable water provision (GlobeScan 2019), and land management policy for natural carbon sequestration in forests (Ni et al 2016).

Direct measures and policy proxies

In designing policy indicators we opt for the most direct measurement of policy for which reliable data exist. Where possible, direct measurements of policy are used: Biodiversity Protection (the proportion of ecologically important areas protected by law), Tax Revenue (revenue raised as a share of GDP), and the government expenditure component of research and development (the share of GDP spent on public R&D). Direct numerical measurement is possible only when a government controls the level of a single parameter that determines the strength of a policy.

Many of the policies needed for sustainable and shared prosperity are not so simple. Some policy goals require a multifaceted package adapted to local circumstances and constraints, whose parts work together to achieve the goal. In those cases we use outcome metrics as proxies for packages of government policy. Deforestation (percentage change in forest cover against a 1990s benchmark), Fatal Injuries (fatal injuries per ten thousand workers), and Primary School Enrollment (the share of primary-age children enrolled in formal education) are not specified directly by policy in the way public research spending is, yet government policy nevertheless determines their level, especially over the long run. Proxy measures acknowledge the variety of specific policies, each adapted to a particular geographic, cultural, and political context, that countries may pursue toward a universal policy goal.

Proxy indicators require care in their interpretation. Outcome metrics reflect circumstances as well as policy, and the two cannot be fully seperated. We accept this trade-off in instances in which an important policy area can only be imperfectly measured by a proxy.

Organizing indicators into categories and pillars

The three pillars correspond to three functions of government. Public Goods covers goods and services supplied or guaranteed directly by government, which are largely under the direct control of a national or regional administration. Market Structure brings together the policies that regulate and structure how markets function, on the supply side through employment policy, taxation, and the protection of property and competition, and on the demand side through economic security and a financial system that supports the effective operation of markets. The policies in those two pillars do not capture a government's responsibility to protect the environment for people today and for future generations, which is the role of the Sustainability pillar: policies governing the management and use of natural resources and the externalities that follow from degrading a country's natural capital. A firm's production decisions rest on its own costs, which typically exclude external costs to public health or the environment; the public pays those costs instead. The Sustainability pillar measures the extent to which governments have policies in place that internalize them.

Pillars and categories should be read as systems of policies that operate together. Many kinds of policy affect how markets operate, the health of the environment, or the quality of life, and the goal is a set of policies that influence these jointly within the overall socioeconomic system. Infrastructure is an example: access to electricity, water quality, basic sanitation, internet access, and the transport network are commonly built by governments and either operated by them or regulated in private hands, and together they support daily life and shape environmental outcomes.

Every indicator, with its definition, its sources, and its score function, is listed in the indicator table.

{{ section_head('data-sources') }}

In selecting data to represent the policy indicators, and then aggregating them into a composite index, we follow the recommended practice for composite indicators set out by the OECD (OECD 2008). Indicators are built on data that are well defined, reliable, and publicly available over time. We use only credible organizations whose data cover many countries. Objective administrative data is preferred to subjective survey data.

Where two candidate variables might represent the same information, and so be interchangeable, we compare their correlation. When variables are highly correlated and judged to carry the same information, we keep the one with more country observations or higher data quality. We also use sensitivity testing to establish how much an indicator depends on the particular variable chosen and on the way it enters its category.

The index currently draws on {{ dataset_count }} datasets from {{ organization_count }} organizations. A dataset is one measured series from one source, collected and cleaned on its own terms; an indicator is one or more datasets combined by a published score function. Some indicators use a single dataset; others combine several. Biodiversity Protection, for instance, is the average of the protected share of important terrestrial, freshwater, and marine sites, each scored separately first.

Each dataset has its own page recording its source organization, its query, its unit, and the code that collects it. Those pages are reachable from the indicator table, and the cleaned series themselves can be taken whole from the download page.

{{ section_head('scoring') }}

Every indicator is set up so that a higher score represents a policy oriented toward a better outcome, and every indicator is normalized to a value between 0 and 1 to enable aggregation (see below). Normalization uses a pair of goalposts: a lower goalpost representing a hypothetical minimum for the policy variable and an upper goalpost representing a hypothetical maximum. Values beyond either goalpost are censored at that goalpost.

This follows the goalposting used for the Human Development Index, whose dimension indices (the equivalent of our indicator scores) are normalized to values between 0 and 1 against minima and maxima set from both historical evidence and achievability.

For some policy variables a higher value indicates weaker policy: the homicide rate, the Gini coefficient, and energy intensity are examples. The SSPI inverts these indicators by placing the larger value in the lower-goalpost position. The "lower" in lower goalpost corresponds to the direction indicating poorer policy, which does not necessarily correspond to the magnitude of the value.

Setting Goalposts

Setting the goalposts of an indicator shifts the center and controls the spread of its score distribution while preserving the ranks in the observed data. Where an internationally established norm exists, we use it; several goalposts follow the 2022 Sustainable Development Report (Sachs et al 2022). Where no norm exists, the historical values observed across countries guide what is possible for high performers while leaving room for future improvement, and also benchmark weak performance. We use sensitivity testing on the goalposts of an indicator to examine the distribution and its outliers across countries, and take observed high values as a benchmark of what good policy can achieve.

For biodiversity policy, benchmarks can be drawn from environmental science. To protect the planet's ecosystems, nations must stop causing extinctions and preserve species. The share of important sites covered by protected areas runs from 0 to 100 percent of internationally recognized sites, and a goal of 100 percent is reasonable, since ideally a country's policy would protect every known important site.

Carbon sequestration is a more complex case. We do not know the maximum amount of carbon a country could sequester, because it varies widely with geography and cross-country data are sparse. Without clear benchmarks, we rely on the data itself, land characteristics and historical trends, to set goalposts for what good policy can achieve. Country policy is ranked on carbon density, the ratio of carbon stock in living biomass to forest land, measured against that country's own 1990s average, when deforestation became conspicuous. Higher values indicate that a country is allocating land to forests that sequester carbon. The benchmark is the country's own land endowment. Deforestation follows the same logic, using the ratio of naturally regenerating forest to the 1990s average.

Both extremely high and extremely low outliers are censored and assigned the value of the nearer goalpost. Setting the lower goalpost too low would penalize countries constrained by resources, geography, or history beyond the reach of policy, so some lower goalposts deliberately sit above the worst observed value, both to suppress noise and to mark a reasonable floor for weak policy. A few very high values were judged to be noise and are capped at the upper goalpost. This mirrors the SDG Index, which sets upper targets from known goals or from the average of top performers, sets lower targets at the 2.5th percentile, and censors each distribution at both ends.

One pair of goalposts applies to every country and every year, so a score of 0.60 has the same meaning in {{ year_start }} as in {{ year_end }}. Fixed goalposts make the panel comparable over time. Revising a goalpost changes every score in the history of the indicator, so goalposts are revised only with a documented rationale.

{{ section_head('imputation') }}

Source series rarely cover every country in every year. Without treatment of the gaps, the index would be undefined wherever a single contributing series began late, ended early, or was missing, and countries would enter and leave the ranking for reasons unrelated to their policies. Imputation fills those gaps so that each indicator has a complete set of country-year observations from {{ year_start }} to {{ year_end }}.

Three methods are applied, in this order:

  • Backward extrapolation carries the earliest observed value back to the start of the panel.
  • Forward extrapolation carries the latest observed value forward to the end of the panel.
  • Linear interpolation fills gaps between two observations by drawing a straight line between them.

Imputed values are then scored with the same goalposts as observed values, and stored separately from them, so that any number in the index can be traced back to whether it was measured or inferred. Indicators with complete coverage are not imputed.

Flat extrapolation assumes that policy did not change outside the observed range. The assumption is conservative, and it understates real change at the ends of a series. A long run of extrapolated years should be read as an assumption about policy, and the record of which values were measured and which were imputed is kept for that reason.

After imputation, a country-year receives an SSPI score only if every indicator is present. A country missing any indicator in a year is excluded from the index for that year, because a score computed over a partial tree would not be comparable with the others.

{{ section_head('aggregation') }}

Aggregation is deliberately simple in the SSPI. All scores for index items are between zero and one, and each level of the index is an unweighted arithmetic mean of the components of the level below it.

Equal Weighting: Interpretive Caveats

We use equal weights at each level of the SSPI because they are easy to understand, widely used in the literature, and support the design and interpretability goals of the SSPI.

We recognize that equal-weight aggregation at each level is arbitrary, imposing uneven weighting at the overall index level because of variation in the indicator-per-category count and category-per-pillar count. This variation proved unavoidable in the construction of the index, so, rather than allowing low data availability in one category to constrain all others, we allow the count of indicators and categories to vary, and accept the cost of heterogeneity in weighting at the overall level.

Weighting decisions carry implications for "importance" in the construction of a policy index. Equal weights within a category imply that all policy indicators are equally important within the category, just as equal weights within a pillar imply that all categories are equally important within the pillar, and even weighting across the three pillars implies that each has equal importance. We do not believe we are in a position to evaluate scientifically the commensurability of indicators, categories, and pillars, and still less in a position to encode such a judgement into prescriptive, universally valid weights.

Nevertheless, we do believe that the SSPI identifies and measures a bundle of policies which are universally applicable, should a country want to deliver sustainable and shared prosperity, and we do want to compute summary metrics in the form of aggregate scores which convey real information about the underlying policies. We believe the equal-weight construction is the most defensible way to do so: it makes the bias of aggregation transparent and easy to account for, and it is minimally contaminated with methodological judgements carrying a scientific imprimatur.

Equal weighting also reflects our assumption that the SSPI integrates a system of interrelated and indivisible policies that are equally important. The SDG Index takes the same position, treating each Sustainable Development Goal equally with fixed equal weights.

The table states the cost of the equal-weight construction. Adding an indicator to a category reduces the weight of every indicator already in it, and splitting a category in two doubles the weight of its contents. Because the weights follow from the structure of the tree, every weighting judgement in the index is visible in the indicator table.

We tested the sensitivity of the results to the weights at both levels of aggregation, pillars and categories, by comparing country rankings under alternative schemes, including unequal weights and the geometric average, with the rankings under equal weighting. The tests showed little difference in rankings or scores between the geometric and simple averages. The custom index builder allows readers to extend these tests: drop indicators, move them between categories, split or merge categories, or change score functions, then score the modified tree against the same underlying data and compare the result with the published index.

The published index scores a country-year only when every indicator is present, while the custom builder averages whichever children are available. A custom tree with sparse coverage therefore still produces a score, and that score is an average over a different set of indicators for different countries.

{{ section_head('limitations') }}

The following limitations bear on the interpretation of SSPI scores.

  • Proxy indicators reflect circumstance as well as policy. Where an indicator is an outcome metric standing in for a package of policies, its level depends on geography and history as well as on government action. Forest cover reflects terrain and settlement history as well as land management.
  • Equal weighting implies unequal indicator weights. As the table above shows, the implied weight of an indicator varies by a factor of about {{ spread }} with its position in the tree.
  • Censoring removes information at the extremes. Every country beyond a goalpost receives the same score of 0 or 1, so the index does not distinguish among leaders or among laggards on an indicator where many countries are censored.
  • Imputed years are assumptions. Flat extrapolation across a long gap shows a country's policy as unchanged when it may have changed.
  • Findings are specific to the panel. The {{ country_count }} countries are large, industrialized economies. Relationships found in this panel need not hold elsewhere.
  • Small differences in score lie within measurement error. Scores inherit the measurement error of every source series beneath them. Gaps of a few hundredths, and the rank changes that follow from them, should not be treated as findings.
  • Indicator selection encodes a view of good policy. Deciding that a policy area belongs in the index, and which measurable feature represents it, is a judgement of the research team. Those judgements are documented on the indicator and dataset pages.
  • Source data are revised. Statistical agencies restate historical series. A score can change between collections without any change in policy, so each published figure is tied to the collection that produced it.
{{ section_head('reproducibility') }}

Early versions of the SSPI were built in spreadsheets. Data were copied by hand from source websites into worksheets, indicators were computed in separate workbooks, and results were copied by hand into a main file. The only check on the procedure was to repeat it by hand and compare the results.

Two classes of error recurred. Rankings were often produced by sorting a selected range, and a selection that was slightly wrong scrambled countries against their values. The scoring scheme was distributed across dozens of sheets, so determining which indicators entered a category required auditing every formula in every row. Both classes of error were hard to detect and costly to correct.

Rewriting the index as code addressed the underlying problem, because code makes each procedure explicit. Sources and transformations are named once and run repeatedly, and a mistake is a mistake in a written procedure that can be found and corrected. The 2018 index was frozen only after it had been reproduced in code, and the reproduction caught several substantive errors in earlier versions.

Data now move through the system in stages: collected from the source API, cleaned into a standard form, imputed where necessary, scored against goalposts, and finalized into item scores. Every stage is stored, so any number on this site can be traced back to the response returned by the source organization.
